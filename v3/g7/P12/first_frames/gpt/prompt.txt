Use case: scientific-educational
Asset type: photorealistic INCIDENT-ONLY first-frame reference for an image-to-video refraction benchmark

Temporal role — critical:
This frame shows only the incoming laser beams arriving at one flat water-air interface. No refracted beam, no reflected beam and no beam continuation past the interface may appear anywhere in this image. Every beam simply stops dead at the surface. The outgoing rays are what the later video has to produce, so drawing any of them here destroys the task.

Primary request:
Photograph a rectangular transparent glass tank, half filled with perfectly still clear water, so that one single flat horizontal water-air interface runs straight across the frame. Four thin, sharply defined laser beams lie in one common vertical plane just inside the front glass wall and each one terminates exactly on that interface. Each beam is a different saturated colour so it can be tracked individually.

Beam geometry — the four incidence angles must be clearly and visibly different:
1. A CYAN beam comes down through the air from the upper left and strikes the interface at a shallow angle of about 20 degrees from the vertical surface normal, so it looks nearly vertical.
2. A GREEN beam comes down through the air and strikes the interface at about 40 degrees from the normal, so it is clearly slanted.
3. An AMBER beam comes down through the air and strikes the interface at about 60 degrees from the normal, so it is much closer to horizontal than to vertical.
4. A MAGENTA beam travels upward inside the water from the lower right and strikes the interface from below at about 49 degrees from the normal. This one is critical and is usually drawn too steeply: the magenta beam must be much closer to HORIZONTAL than to vertical. For every 1 unit it rises through the water it must travel about 1.15 units sideways, so its path is a long shallow slant across the water body rather than a near-vertical climb. It must look at least as slanted as the amber beam and clearly far more slanted than the cyan beam. A steep, near-vertical magenta beam makes the frame useless.
The three downward air beams hit the interface at three well-separated points spread across the width of the tank, and the fourth hit point is separated from all of them. Because 20, 40 and 60 degrees are far apart, an observer must be able to tell the three air beams apart by their slope alone at a glance; do not let any two of them look like the same angle. Ordered by slant, from most nearly vertical to most nearly horizontal, the four beams must read: cyan, then green, then amber and magenta.

Surface normals:
At each of the four hit points, draw one thin pale dashed line perpendicular to the water surface, extending a short distance both above and below the interface, with a small clean gap exactly at the surface. Each dashed normal is centred on its own hit point.

Scene/backdrop:
A dim optics laboratory. Clean thick-walled rectangular glass tank on a dark matte optical bench. Deep near-black charcoal background so the coloured beams and the water surface both read with strong contrast. Faint haze inside the tank and faint haze in the air above it, just enough to make each beam a continuous visible line.

The water body itself must be unmistakable. Give the water a clear, even pale blue-green tint and a soft internal luminosity, so the lower half of the tank is a visibly brighter, visibly coloured block sitting under the dark air in the upper half. An observer glancing at the frame must be able to say where the water is without following the beams. The boundary between the dark air above and the tinted water below is therefore a strong, bright, razor-sharp horizontal edge running unbroken from the left inner wall to the right inner wall, and it is the highest-contrast horizontal feature in the image. The water is clean and completely motionless and that surface line is perfectly flat and level.

Composition/framing:
Locked-off exact front view, orthographic-looking, optical axis perpendicular to the common beam plane, so no beam is foreshortened and no measured angle is distorted by perspective. Horizontal 16:9 frame. The flat interface runs horizontally across roughly the middle of the frame. Keep the whole tank, the full interface, all four incident beams, all four hit points and all four dashed normals inside the frame. Leave generous empty space in the water below the interface and in the air above it, because that is where the later outgoing beams will appear.

Lighting/mood:
Dark ambient with soft grazing light on the glass edges only. Beams are self-luminous, thin, even in thickness and free of bloom, flare, glow halos and starburst artefacts. No caustic patterns on the tank floor and no bright reflections that could be mistaken for a beam.

Materials/textures:
Real optical glass with believable edge reflections, clean still water with a slightly thick meniscus at the side walls, thin crisp laser lines. Restrained documentary realism at real laboratory scale.

Initial-state constraints:
The water is at rest, the interface is exactly flat and horizontal, and all four beams are steady. Each beam is a single straight segment ending precisely at its own hit point on the interface. No beam crosses another beam. No beam passes through the interface, bends at the interface, or reappears on the other side.

Measurement-ready presentation:
Each beam must be one clean straight high-contrast line of constant width against the dark scene, wide enough to survive line fitting but thin enough to have a well-defined axis. The interface must be one straight unbroken high-contrast horizontal edge. The four hit points must be clearly separated from one another and each must be unobstructed.

Real-world capture priority:
The result must look like one unedited frame captured by a real fixed laboratory camera during an actual university optics experiment, not a diagram, product render, simulation, or synthetic CGI scene. Preserve believable material microtexture, ordinary optical imperfections, physically consistent reflections, restrained lens response, and natural sensor-level tonal variation. Keep the scene clean enough for computer vision because the real apparatus is well designed and well lit; never use digital edge enhancement, artificial contour drawing, measurement overlays, or textbook-like simplification.

Avoid:
any refracted beam, any reflected beam, any beam continuing below or above the interface, beams of the same colour, three air beams at similar angles, a steep or near-vertical magenta beam inside the water, a magenta beam less slanted than the green beam, water that is the same brightness as the air above it, a faint or barely visible water surface line, a curved or wavy water surface, ripples, waves, bubbles, cloudy or coloured water, a second interface, a lens, a prism, a mirror, laser housings or emitters inside the beam plane, beam glow halos, lens flare, bloom, perspective tilt of the water line, angle marks, protractors, rulers, scale bars, labels, numbers, arrows, formulas, axes, captions, logos, watermarks, split panels, before/after images, people, hands, cartoon styling, toy styling, or any written characters.
